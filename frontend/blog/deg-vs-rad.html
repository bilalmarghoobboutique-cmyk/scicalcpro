DEG vs RAD: Complete Guide to Angle Modes on Scientific Calculator

📅 08 October 2026 ⏱️ 6 min read 👤 Bilal Marghoob
📢 Advertisement
Responsive — In-Content

If you've ever calculated sin(30) and got a completely different answer than expected, your calculator was probably in the wrong angle mode. DEG and RAD are the two most common angle modes, and using the wrong one can completely change your result. This guide explains the difference and when to use each.

1. What is an Angle?

An angle measures the rotation between two lines that share a common point (the vertex). Angles are used everywhere — from architecture to astronomy to video game graphics.

There are three common ways to measure angles: degrees, radians, and gradians. Each divides a full circle differently.

2. Degrees (DEG) — The Everyday Mode

Degrees are the most familiar unit. A full circle is divided into 360 degrees.

  • Full circle = 360°
  • Half circle (straight line) = 180°
  • Right angle = 90°
  • Equilateral triangle angle = 60°

Why 360? It's believed to come from ancient Babylonians, who used a base-60 number system. 360 has many divisors (2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12...), making it convenient for fractions.

Example: sin(30°) = 0.5 (in DEG mode)

3. Radians (RAD) — The Mathematical Mode

Radians are the "natural" unit for angles in advanced mathematics. A full circle is 2π radians (approximately 6.283).

  • Full circle = 2π radians
  • Half circle = π radians
  • Right angle = π/2 radians
  • Equilateral triangle = π/3 radians

Why radians? In calculus, physics, and engineering, radians simplify many formulas. For example, the derivative of sin(x) is cos(x) only when x is in radians.

Example: sin(π/6) = 0.5 (in RAD mode)

4. Gradians (GRAD) — The Rare Mode

Gradians (also called "gons") divide a full circle into 400 gradians. This makes a right angle exactly 100 gradians.

  • Full circle = 400 gradians
  • Right angle = 100 gradians
  • Half circle = 200 gradians

Gradians are used mostly in surveying and some European engineering fields. Most students will never need them.

5. Conversion Formulas

To convert between the three modes:

  • Degrees → Radians: rad = deg × (π / 180)
  • Radians → Degrees: deg = rad × (180 / π)
  • Degrees → Gradians: grad = deg × (10 / 9)
  • Gradians → Degrees: deg = grad × (9 / 10)

Examples:

  • 30° = 30 × (π / 180) = π/6 ≈ 0.5236 rad
  • π/4 rad = (π/4) × (180/π) = 45°
  • 90° = 90 × (10/9) = 100 grad

6. When to Use Each Mode

Use DEG when:

  • You're doing basic trigonometry (school level)
  • Angles are given in degrees (30°, 45°, 90°)
  • Surveying, navigation, and everyday problems
  • Most engineering problems in Pakistan/India use DEG

Use RAD when:

  • You're doing calculus (derivatives, integrals)
  • Physics problems (angular velocity, wave equations)
  • Programming and computer graphics
  • Angles are given in terms of π

Use GRAD when:

  • Surveying (rare)
  • Specific engineering fields in Europe

7. Real Examples

Example 1: Calculate sin(30)

  • In DEG mode: sin(30) = 0.5 ✅
  • In RAD mode: sin(30) = -0.988 ❌ (wrong if you wanted degrees)

Example 2: Calculate cos(π)

  • In RAD mode: cos(π) = -1 ✅
  • In DEG mode: cos(π) ≈ cos(3.14°) = 0.998 ❌ (wrong)

This is why checking the mode before every trigonometric calculation is critical.

8. Common Mistakes to Avoid

  1. Not checking the mode — The #1 mistake. Always check before sin, cos, tan.
  2. Mixing modes in one calculation — Don't switch midway.
  3. Forgetting π — In RAD mode, π is a number (3.14159...). Type it, don't ignore it.
  4. Assuming default is DEG — Some calculators default to RAD. Always verify.
  5. Using RAD for school trig — Unless specified, use DEG for school problems.

Conclusion

DEG, RAD, and GRAD are three ways to measure the same thing — angles. The choice depends on your field and the problem. For school math, stick with DEG. For advanced math and physics, switch to RAD.
